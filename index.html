Built for first-time users

College Tour VR

A campus you can walk through from four hundred kilometres away.

A five-person project in which I built several of the 3D campus environment modules and handled the integration into VR for headset delivery. Almost everyone using it would be wearing a headset for the first time, so the environments had to be legible before anyone understood the controls.

Read the full story

Choosing a college in India often means committing to a place you have never stood in. Photographs flatten distance; a corridor looks the same length in every brochure.

This is that campus rebuilt so a prospective student can walk it before they ever arrive — down the corridors, into the labs, across the ground.

My part was the environments and getting the whole thing running on a headset. I built several of the campus modules and handled the VR integration, which meant every decision I made about a space was really a decision about whether a first-time user could read it.

Spatial legibility turned out to be the problem, not fidelity. A corridor that is accurate to the centimetre is still useless if you cannot tell where it leads, and someone wearing a headset for the first time will not explore a space they cannot parse. So sightlines mattered more than detail: keeping landmarks visible from the entry points, and making sure that turning around always showed you something you recognised.

As a team we kept the campus open rather than guided. If the point is to decide whether you want to be in a place, being walked through it on rails defeats the purpose — so the work had to make the space self-explanatory instead.

The project received a Gold Medal for innovation and execution, and the research was published; I am fifth author and presented it.

Seven Wonders WebAR

Seven wonders of the world, standing in your room, with nothing to install.

Try it now ↗

Opens in your phone's browser. No app, no headset.

Most AR asks for an app first. This runs in the browser: open a link, point your phone at the floor, and the Taj Mahal is standing in your room. The models stay light enough to load over a phone connection, and the monument only appears once the floor is found.

Read the full story

Most augmented reality asks for a download first. For a child, that means asking a parent for the phone, the password, and permission — three obstacles before anything appears.

This runs in a browser. You open a link, point your phone at the floor, and the Taj Mahal is standing in the room in front of you.

Working without an app imposes real limits. Everything has to be small enough to load over a phone connection, and browser tracking is less stable than a native app. So the models are kept light and the scenes simple: nothing that would push the file size up or the frame rate down. And the monument only appears once the floor has been found, so nobody ever sees it floating in mid-air.

That constraint turned out to be the right one. The thing people remember is scale — a monument sitting in a room at the wrong size — and scale survives a low-poly model perfectly well.

AR Visiting Card

A paper card that answers back.

Point a phone at an ordinary printed card and a 3D character stands up on it, with contact buttons anchored beside them. No app, no sign-up — the camera is the only thing anyone needs.

Try it now ↗

Read the full story

A business card has about four seconds of attention before it goes into a pocket and is forgotten. The question was whether those four seconds could carry more than a phone number.

Image tracking made the card itself the marker, so nothing has to be scanned in a particular way and no QR code sits in the middle of the design. You point the camera and the card is recognised.

The constraint was the same one as Seven Wonders: it has to run in a browser on a phone someone already has in their hand. That rules out anything heavy, so the character is light and the interaction is limited to the things a person would actually want in that moment — call, message, open a link.

Training and high-stakes work

Weber Rescue

Training for the tools that cut a car open, without cutting a car open.

Delivered · under client review

Weber Rescue makes the hydraulic tools used to open a vehicle after a crash, and training on them is expensive and dangerous. I found and pitched this international client myself. The central decision was restraint: the procedure is enforced, so you can't skip to the satisfying part — a worse game, and a better training environment.

Read the full story

Weber Rescue makes hydraulic cutters and spreaders — the equipment used to open a vehicle after a crash. Training on them is expensive and genuinely dangerous: you need a scrap car, a closed site, and a supervisor for every trainee.

I found this client myself and approached them directly with a proposal to move part of that training into VR.

The hard part was restraint. A rescue simulation in VR can easily become a game about cutting things up. But the people using this are learning a procedure where the order of operations is the whole point — stabilise before you cut, and know what is behind the panel you are about to breach.

So the tool behaves correctly and the sequence is enforced. You cannot skip ahead to the satisfying part. That is a worse game and a better training environment, and choosing the second was the central decision of the project.

03

Interaction experiments

Hand Tracking / MR to VR

Putting the controllers down, and letting the room disappear.

Two experiments asking how much equipment you can remove before the experience breaks. One drops the controllers so your own hands do the work; the other fades the real room into a virtual one slowly enough that people arrive somewhere else without noticing they left.

Read the full story

Two experiments that turned out to be the same question: how much of the equipment can you remove before the experience breaks?

The first drops the controllers. Your own hands do the work — reaching, grabbing, pushing. Hand tracking is less precise than a controller, so anything requiring fine aim had to go. What is left is slower and more deliberate, and people stop thinking about their hands within about thirty seconds, which a controller never quite allows.

The second removes the room. You start in mixed reality, seeing the actual space you are standing in, and it fades gradually into a virtual one. No loading screen, no cut to black.

The transition speed was the entire design problem. Fast feels like a trick and makes people flinch. Slow enough and they do not notice it happening, which is the point: you want someone to arrive somewhere else without ever having left.

VR Shooting Game

A shooting range that teaches your hands without telling them anything.

A range with no tutorial and no text. The rule was that nothing could be explained in words: if a player was confused, I changed how the object felt instead of adding a label.

Read the full story

A range with no tutorial, no text prompts and no onboarding sequence. You pick up the weapon and the weight of it tells you how to hold it.

The rule I set myself was that nothing could be explained in words. If a player did not understand something, that was a failure of the object, not of the instructions — so the fix was always to change how the thing felt rather than to add a label.

It is the smallest project here and the one that taught me most about physical feedback. Reloading, recoil and the moment of picking a weapon up are all doing the work that a tutorial would otherwise do.

Objects and product visualisation

3D Modelling

Every object in the work above started as a Blender file.

The monuments, the rescue tools, the props in the campus — I model them myself. Modelling for real time is a different discipline from modelling for a render: the budget is fixed before you start, and the question is always which detail survives being seen from thirty centimetres away on a headset.

Read the full story

A model that lives in a headset has a frame budget. A model that lives in a browser has a download budget as well. Both are decided before the first vertex, and almost every modelling choice after that is about where to spend what is left.

So the method is to find the one feature that carries recognition and protect it. On the Taj Mahal it is the silhouette of the dome against the sky — get that right and a low-poly model reads correctly from across a room. On a hydraulic cutter it is the jaw geometry, because that is what a trainee looks at while deciding where to place it.

Everything else is texture rather than geometry. Baked normal and ambient occlusion maps carry detail that would otherwise cost polygons, which means the same asset can go into a Quest build, a WebXR scene and a high-resolution render without being rebuilt three times.

That single-source pipeline — Blender to glTF, one asset serving a headset, a browser and a still image — is the part I have spent the most time getting right.

Solo
Tools
Blender, glTF, texture baking, PBR materials

Product Visualisation

Showing a product at its real size, in the room it is meant for.

A photograph cannot tell you whether a chair fits your room or whether a finish looks right in your light. Placing a product in the customer's own space, at true scale, answers both in about four seconds — and it runs in a browser, so nobody has to install anything first.

Read the full story

Online, the two questions that stop a purchase are almost always the same: how big is it really, and what does that material actually look like?

Product visualisation in AR answers the first question immediately. The model appears on the customer's own floor at the size it will be, next to furniture they already own. No comparison shot, no tape measure.

The second question is a materials problem rather than a modelling one. A finish that looks right under studio lighting can look plastic in a living room, so the materials have to behave sensibly under whatever light the phone finds, not just under the light I chose.

This is the same browser-based pipeline behind Seven Wonders: a glTF model, no app, a link that opens on any phone. What changes is the standard. A monument only has to feel impressive. A product has to be trusted enough that someone spends money on it.
